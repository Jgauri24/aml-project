\begin{abstract}
Accurate multi-horizon inventory demand forecasting is vital for retail supply-chain efficiency, working-capital optimization, and stockout reduction. Retail sales time series, however, present severe modeling challenges: pervasive intermittency, 69.33\% zero-demand frequency, pronounced weekly calendar cycles, and promotional demand shocks. In this work, we conduct a rigorous comparative evaluation between two contrasting forecasting paradigms: local state-space tracking (Kalman Filtering variants) and global machine learning (Direct Multi-Horizon LightGBM), benchmarked against seasonal heuristic baselines. Experiments are conducted on a deterministic, stratified cohort of 240 Walmart M5 product series over an expanding four-fold rolling-origin backtest ($H=28$ days, 26,880 out-of-sample evaluated predictions), strictly preserving a sealed final holdout. Our empirical findings demonstrate a clear structural trade-off: (1) Global LightGBM achieves superior point accuracy (0.9227 MAE, 0.7640 WAPE) by sharing cross-series promotional and calendar representations across the product hierarchy; (2) Kalman Local Level with Seasonal Harmonics achieves the lowest squared error (2.0535 RMSE), effectively dampening extreme weekend demand spikes; and (3) Local Kalman filters deliver analytically closed-form, well-calibrated 80\% prediction intervals (81.08\% empirical coverage on single SKU, 85.72\% on seasonal variants). We synthesize these findings into an operational dual-model supply-chain framework and an interactive Streamlit diagnostic platform.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Modern supply-chain operations require inventory forecasting systems capable of supporting two distinct decision regimes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Point Demand Sizing:} Accurate conditional expectation or median estimates to optimize procurement batches, warehouse logistics, and replenishment orders.
    \item \textbf{Safety-Stock Sizing:} Calibrated prediction intervals and variance bounds to cushion against demand volatility without incurring excessive inventory holding costs.
\end{enumerate}

Retail daily sales data, exemplified by the Walmart M5 dataset~\cite{b5}, violate standard statistical assumptions. They exhibit high intermittency (frequent zero-demand days), asymmetric promotional spikes driven by Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) benefits, stepped price elasticity, and 7-day calendar seasonality.

In demand forecasting literature, practitioners typically choose between two paradigms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Local Time-Series Models:} E.g., ARIMA, Croston's method, and State-Space Kalman Filters~\cite{b1,b3}. These models fit independent parameters per series, adapting rapidly to local level shifts and natively supporting missing observations. However, they cannot share structural knowledge across sparse series.
    \item \textbf{Global Tabular Machine Learning:} E.g., Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (LightGBM~\cite{b4}) and Deep Neural Networks~\cite{b2}. These pool histories from thousands of items into a unified model, allowing low-velocity products to borrow statistical strength from high-velocity items.
\end{itemize}

This technical report delivers a thorough Phase 1 benchmarking study comparing local state-space Kalman filters against global tabular LightGBM across a deterministic cohort of 240 series from the M5 dataset. We structure our evaluation using a four-fold rolling-origin backtest (26,880 out-of-sample predictions) with strict temporal anti-leakage invariants.

\section{Dataset Architecture \& Cutoff-Safe EDA}

\subsection{The Walmart M5 Dataset \& Cohort Selection}
The canonical data source is the Walmart M5 Hierarchical Sales dataset, comprising daily unit sales across stores in California (CA), Texas (TX), and Wisconsin (WI). To ensure reproducible, computationally tractable, and statistically sound experimentation, we construct a deterministic, stratified cohort of $N=240$ series (Seed 42) via \texttt{src/data/select\_skus.py}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Volume Stratification:} Exactly 80 Low-, 80 Medium-, and 80 High-volume series, partitioned by trailing-365-day sales volume up to cutoff day $d_{1801}$.
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical Diversity:} Spans all 3 states, all 10 Walmart retail stores, all 3 categories (\texttt{FOODS}, \texttt{HOUSEHOLD}, \texttt{HOBBIES}), and all 7 product departments.
    \item \textbf{No Artificial Selection Bias:} No minimum positive-sales rule is applied; genuine intermittent zero-sales series are preserved.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cutoff-Safe Exploratory Data Analysis (Milestone 2)}
To guarantee zero lookahead bias, all exploratory data analysis (EDA) was restricted strictly to historical days $d_1$ through $d_{1801}$ (2011-01-29 to 2016-01-03, comprising 432,240 series-days). Key empirical findings include:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pervasive Intermittency \& Zero-Inflation:} 
    Overall, $69.33\%$ of daily sales records are exact zeros. In the low-volume group, $89.06\%$ of days have zero sales, with a median coefficient of variation ($CV$) of $3.27$ and a maximum observed zero streak of $1,640$ consecutive days.
    \item \textbf{Demand Pattern Classification:} Applying the Syntetos-Boylan demand categorization framework yields:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item 175 Intermittent series (sporadic transaction arrivals)
        \item 54 Lumpy series (sporadic arrivals with volatile non-zero volume)
        \item 8 Smooth series | 3 Erratic series
    \end{itemize}
    Because standard percentage metrics like MAPE diverge when actual sales $y_t = 0$, Weighted Absolute Percentage Error (WAPE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) are mathematically required.
    \item \textbf{7-Day Cyclical Seasonality:} Autocorrelation Function (ACF) analysis reveals pronounced peaks at lags 7, 14, 21, and 28 ($r_7$ median $= 0.1639$; up to $0.3164$ in high-volume SKUs). The weekend-to-weekday peak-to-trough ratio is $1.369\times$, reflecting a $25\%\text{--}40\%$ volume surge on Saturdays and Sundays.
    \item \textbf{SNAP Assistance Demand Shocks:} Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) disbursements during the first 10 days of each month induce measurable, state-specific demand lifts:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item California: $+4.8\%$ lift (Disbursement ratio $1.048$)
        \item Texas: $+6.0\%$ lift (Disbursement ratio $1.060$)
        \item Wisconsin: $+8.0\%$ lift (Disbursement ratio $1.080$)
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Price Dynamics:} Prices exhibit stepped, infrequent reductions. The median price-demand elasticity correlation is $-0.0471$, with temporary rollbacks inducing sharp volume spikes.
\end{enumerate}

\section{State-Space Modeling \& Kalman Filtering}

State-space formulations treat observed daily sales $y_t$ as an indirect, noisy reflection of an underlying latent demand state $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t$.

\subsection{General Linear Gaussian State-Space System}
The state-space model is defined by observation and state transition equations:
\begin{align}
y_t &= \mathbf{Z}_t \boldsymbol{\alpha}_t + \varepsilon_t, \quad &\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R) \label{eq:gen_obs} \\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t &= \mathbf{T}_t \boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t-1} + \boldsymbol{\eta}_t, \quad &\boldsymbol{\eta}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{Q}) \label{eq:gen_state}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{Z}_t$ is the observation selection vector, $\mathbf{T}_t$ is the state transition matrix, $R > 0$ is observation variance, and $\mathbf{Q} \ge 0$ is the process noise covariance matrix.

\subsection{The Evaluated Kalman Variants (Milestones 6 \& 7)}
We developed and evaluated five distinct per-series state-space models:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single-SKU Baseline (\texttt{kalman\_single\_sku}):} The Milestone 6 reference local-level model. Scalar state $\alpha_t = [\mu_t]$, with transition $T = [1]$ and observation $Z = [1]$.
    \item \textbf{Local Level (\texttt{kalman\_local\_level}):} Standard random walk level:
    \begin{equation}
    y_t = \mu_t + \varepsilon_t, \quad \mu_t = \mu_{t-1} + \eta_t
    \end{equation}
    where $\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R)$ and $\eta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q_{\text{level}})$.
    \item \textbf{Local Level + Seasonal (\texttt{kalman\_local\_level\_seasonal}):} Augments the level state with a 6-dimensional cyclical seasonal vector $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_t$:
    \begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\alpha}_t = [\mu_t, \gamma_{t,1}, \dots, \gamma_{t,6}]^T, \quad \mathbf{Z} = [1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0]
    \end{equation}
    The seasonal transition block $\mathbf{S}_{6 \times 6}$ has ones along the superdiagonal and an all-$-1$ final row, enforcing the harmonic condition $\sum_{j=0}^6 \gamma_{t-j} = 0$.
    \item \textbf{Local Trend (\texttt{kalman\_local\_trend}):} Incorporates a local growth slope $\beta_t$ with damping factor $\rho = 0.98$:
    \begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\alpha}_t = \begin{bmatrix} \mu_t \\ \beta_t \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & \rho \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{Z} = [1, 0]
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Local Trend + Seasonal (\texttt{kalman\_local\_trend\_seasonal}):} Full structural model combining damped trend and weekly harmonics ($8$-dimensional state space).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Recursive Filtering Algorithm \& Missing Data}
Let $\mathbf{a}_{t|t-1} = \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]$ and $\mathbf{P}_{t|t-1} = \text{Var}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1})$. Given diffuse prior variance $\mathbf{P}_0 = 10^4 \cdot \mathbf{I}$, the filter executes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Time Update (Predict):}
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{a}_{t|t-1} = \mathbf{T} \mathbf{a}_{t-1|t-1}, \quad \mathbf{P}_{t|t-1} = \mathbf{T} \mathbf{P}_{t-1|t-1} \mathbf{T}^T + \mathbf{Q}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Measurement Update (Correct):}
    When $y_t$ is observed:
    \begin{align}
    v_t &= y_t - \mathbf{Z} \mathbf{a}_{t|t-1} \quad (\text{Innovation}) \\
    S_t &= \mathbf{Z} \mathbf{P}_{t|t-1} \mathbf{Z}^T + R \quad (\text{Innovation Variance}) \\
    \mathbf{K}_t &= \mathbf{P}_{t|t-1} \mathbf{Z}^T S_t^{-1} \quad (\text{Kalman Gain}) \\
    \mathbf{a}_{t|t} &= \mathbf{a}_{t|t-1} + \mathbf{K}_t v_t \\
    \mathbf{P}_{t|t} &= (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}_t \mathbf{Z}) \mathbf{P}_{t|t-1}
    \end{align}
    \item \textbf{Stockout / Missing Data Invariant:}
    If sales $y_t$ are unobserved or censored (`\texttt{NaN}'), the measurement update is bypassed entirely:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{a}_{t|t} = \mathbf{a}_{t|t-1}, \quad \mathbf{P}_{t|t} = \mathbf{P}_{t|t-1}
    \end{equation}
    The filter coasts forward on its transition model without fabricating zeros.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Parameter Estimation via MLE}
Hyperparameters $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\ln Q, \ln R)$ are estimated per-series by minimizing the exact prediction-error negative log-likelihood:
\begin{equation}
\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^T \left[ \ln(2\pi S_t) + \frac{v_t^2}{S_t} \right]
\end{equation}
Optimization is executed via L-BFGS-B with multiple restarts in log-variance space ($\theta \in [-10, 8]$) to avoid local minima.

\subsection{Multi-Step Predictive Distribution}
For horizon $h \in \{1,\dots,H\}$ from origin $T$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conditional Point Expectation:} For the local-level model, $\mathbb{E}[y_{T+h} \mid \mathcal{F}_T] = \mu_{T|T}$ (invariant across horizons due to the random-walk expectation).
    \item \textbf{Predictive Variance Accumulation:}
    \begin{equation}
    V_h = \text{Var}(y_{T+h} \mid \mathcal{F}_T) = \mathbf{Z} \mathbf{P}_{T+h|T} \mathbf{Z}^T + R = P_{T|T} + h \cdot Q + R
    \end{equation}
    Uncertainty expands linearly with forecast horizon step $h$.
    \item \textbf{Zero-Clipped Quantiles:}
    \begin{equation}
    \hat{q}_\tau(y_{T+h}) = \max\left(0, \hat{y}_{T+h} + z_\tau \sqrt{V_h}\right)
    \end{equation}
    where $z_\tau$ is the standard normal critical value ($z_{0.10} = -1.282, z_{0.90} = +1.282$).
\end{itemize}

\section{Feature Engineering \& Global LightGBM}

\subsection{Cross-Series Statistical Learning}
While Kalman filters estimate independent parameters per series, a global model pools all 240 series into a unified tabular space. This allows low-velocity products to borrow promotional, calendar, and seasonal elasticities learned from dense items.

\subsection{Leakage-Safe Feature Pipeline (Milestone 8)}
To prevent lookahead bias, the feature engineering pipeline (\texttt{src/features/feature\_engineering.py}) constructs 26 features strictly anchored at forecast origin $T$ ($t \le T$):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Demand Lags:} Exact sales at $T-1, T-7, T-14, T-28$.
    \item \textbf{Rolling Statistics:} 7-day and 28-day rolling means and standard deviations over $[T-W, T]$.
    \item \textbf{Calendar Encodings:} Day-of-week, day-of-month, month, weekend indicator, and circular sine/cosine transforms.
    \item \textbf{Exogenous Indicators:} State SNAP indicators (\texttt{snap\_CA}, \texttt{snap\_TX}, \texttt{snap\_WI}), national event flags, and sell prices.
    \item \textbf{Direct Horizon Invariant:} For forecast horizon $h$, any lag feature with lag $< h$ is set to \texttt{NaN} and processed via LightGBM's native missingness branching. The integrity is verified via \texttt{assert\_no\_future\_leakage()}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{LightGBM Forecaster Architecture (Milestone 9)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Loss Function:} Trained with L1 loss (\texttt{regression\_l1} / MAE), forcing trees to predict the conditional median. This guarantees high resilience against zero-inflation and holiday demand spikes.
    \item \textbf{Rolling Validation Tuning:} Tuned over the first 3 backtest folds. Selected optimal configuration: \texttt{learning\_rate}: $0.05$, \texttt{n\_estimators}: $500$, \texttt{num\_leaves}: $31$, \texttt{feature\_fraction}: $0.8$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Protocol \& Backtesting}

\subsection{Four-Fold Chronological Backtesting}
We implement an expanding-window rolling backtest over four historical origins with horizon $H=28$ days:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fold 1:} Origin $d_{1801}$ (2016-01-04 to 2016-01-31, 6,720 forecasts)
    \item \textbf{Fold 2:} Origin $d_{1829}$ (2016-02-01 to 2016-02-28, 6,720 forecasts)
    \item \textbf{Fold 3:} Origin $d_{1857}$ (2016-02-29 to 2016-03-27, 6,720 forecasts)
    \item \textbf{Fold 4:} Origin $d_{1885}$ (2016-03-28 to 2016-04-24, 6,720 forecasts)
\end{itemize}
Total evaluated out-of-sample predictions: $240 \times 4 \times 28 = \mathbf{26,880}$. The final competition period ($d_{1914}\text{--}d_{1941}$) was strictly preserved as a sealed holdout.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE):} $\frac{1}{M}\sum |y_t - \hat{y}_t|$
    \item \textbf{Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):} $\sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum (y_t - \hat{y}_t)^2}$
    \item \textbf{Weighted Absolute Percentage Error (WAPE):}
    \begin{equation}
    \text{WAPE} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^M |y_t - \hat{y}_t|}{\sum_{i=1}^M y_t}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Pinball Loss ($\text{PL}_\tau$):} $\max(\tau(y - \hat{q}_\tau), (1-\tau)(\hat{q}_\tau - y))$
    \item \textbf{Empirical Coverage ($\text{PICP}_{80}$):} Percentage of ground-truth observations within $[\hat{q}_{0.10}, \hat{q}_{0.90}]$ (Nominal: $80\%$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Pooled Performance Analysis}
Table~\ref{tab:master_benchmark} summarizes the primary benchmark results across all 26,880 predictions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Point Accuracy Victory for LightGBM:} Global LightGBM achieves the best overall MAE ($0.9227$) and WAPE ($0.7640$), outperforming the best naive heuristic by \textbf{26.1\%} and the best Kalman model by \textbf{7.8\%}. Cross-series sharing enables effective learning of promotional (SNAP) and calendar dynamics.
    \item \textbf{Squared Error Victory for Seasonal Kalman:} \texttt{kalman\_local\_level\_seasonal} achieves the best overall RMSE ($2.0535$), outperforming even LightGBM ($2.1081$). By tracking weekly harmonic states, the Kalman filter avoids the volatile tail errors produced by tree splits on high-volume weekend sales.
    \item \textbf{Uncertainty Quantification:} The single-SKU Kalman model delivers an empirical coverage of \textbf{81.08\%} for its nominal 80\% interval.
    \item \textbf{Trend Degeneracy on Retail Data:} Models with trend components (\texttt{kalman\_local\_trend}) degraded WAPE to $0.8466$. Retail demand fluctuates cyclically rather than drifting monotonically; trend states overfit temporary demand spikes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Performance Breakdown by Volume Band}
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:volume_band}, product velocity dramatically impacts relative model superiority:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High-Volume Series:} Kalman Local Level + Seasonal achieves WAPE $0.7166$, effectively capturing regular day-of-week consumption patterns.
    \item \textbf{Low-Volume Series:} Intermittent series exhibit high WAPE ($> 1.20$) across all models due to the small denominator. Here, LightGBM dominates because local MLE cannot reliably estimate $(Q, R)$ when $89\%$ of days are zero.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion \& Operational Recommendations}
This benchmark demonstrates that neither local state-space models nor global tree-based forecasters represent a standalone solution for retail supply chains. Instead, our findings support a \textbf{hybrid operational policy}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Procurement Baseline:} Deploy Global LightGBM for baseline purchase order sizing, achieving the lowest expected unit error (0.7640 WAPE).
    \item \textbf{Safety-Stock Sizing:} Utilize Kalman State-Space predictive variance $V_h$ to determine dynamic, calibrated safety stock buffers (81.08\% empirical coverage).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Phase 2 Roadmap:} Future development will integrate these modalities into global probabilistic recurrent architectures (DeepAR~\cite{b2} and Temporal Fusion Transformers), utilizing Kalman filtered states as dynamic structural inputs.
